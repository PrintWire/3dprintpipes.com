The traditional model for sourcing industrial components is to manufacture centrally, warehouse regionally and ship on demand. Large-format 3D printing industrial production distributed manufacturing on-demand parts offers a different model: move fabrication closer to the point of use and hold some parts as digital files instead of physical stock.

The printing side of this is established. Wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) has produced a 12-meter steel bridge and a class-approved ship's propeller, and directed energy deposition (DED) machines with meter-scale work envelopes are sold commercially. The harder question for industrial operators is how to qualify the parts and fit the process into an existing supply chain.

---

## How Large-Format Metal Printing Works

### Wire Arc Additive Manufacturing (WAAM)

WAAM is a variant of directed energy deposition that uses an arc welding process to 3D print metal parts. An electric arc melts wire feedstock, and the process is typically carried by a robotic arm, so part size is limited by the robot's reach. Welding wire is significantly less expensive than the metal powder used in powder bed fusion, and WAAM can work with a wide range of metals provided they are available as wire.

Machine builders include GEFERTEC, a German company that sells a series of WAAM machines for large-scale metal parts, and WAAM3D, a spin-out from Cranfield University whose RoboWAAM machine was first commissioned in 2020. WAAM3D lists titanium, steel, bronze, nickel and aluminium among the materials the process can use.

WAAM typically produces near-net-shape parts with a poor surface finish, so the surface needs to be finished by machining. The high temperatures involved also build up residual stress in the part.

### Directed Energy Deposition (DED)

DED uses a focused energy source, such as a plasma arc, laser or electron beam, to melt material as it is deposited. Feedstock can be powder or wire. According to TWI, powder gives greater accuracy in deposition while wire is more efficient in material use, and almost any weldable metal can be processed. Laser systems use a fully inert chamber for reactive metals, and electron beam systems work in a vacuum.

DED is widely used to repair parts or add material to existing components, and it can produce relatively large parts with minimal tooling. DMG MORI's LASERTEC DED hybrid machines, which combine laser deposition welding and conventional machining in one workspace, take workpieces from 500 mm in diameter on the smallest model up to 1,010 mm in diameter and 3,702 mm long on the largest.

## The Distributed Manufacturing Case

Spare parts for long-lived industrial equipment are often needed rarely and at short notice. Producing them near the point of use, from a qualified digital file, is the idea behind distributed additive manufacturing. Navies offer documented examples.

The U.S. Navy installed a Phillips Additive Hybrid system aboard USS Bataan, described at the time as the first Naval Sea Systems Command sponsored hybrid system permanently installed on a U.S. Navy ship. It puts a Meltio wire-laser metal deposition head on a Haas TM-1 CNC mill and prints 316L stainless steel.

In the United Kingdom, the Submarine Delivery Group announced in June 2026 that shipping containers had arrived at HM Naval Base Clyde to provide an on-site additive manufacturing capability, including metal printing, scanning equipment and engineering workspace. That capability is at the base, not aboard a vessel.

## Applications in Infrastructure and Marine

### Structural Steel

MX3D printed a 12-meter stainless steel pedestrian bridge using robotic WAAM. It was installed over a canal in Amsterdam's historic city center and opened to the public in 2021, carrying sensors that collect structural measurements such as strain, displacement and vibration. MX3D notes that additional time was needed for structural testing, sensor integration and approvals.

### Marine Components

The WAAMpeller, unveiled in 2017, was presented as the world's first class-approved 3D-printed ship's propeller. RAMLAB fabricated it by WAAM from 298 layers of nickel aluminium bronze alloy to a triple-blade design by Promarin, with Bureau Veritas verifying the development, production and testing process. Autodesk and Damen were also partners.

### Energy and Process Equipment

WAAM suppliers are targeting the energy sector directly. GEFERTEC says its machines are in operation from the energy sector to aerospace, WAAM3D lists oil, gas, renewables and nuclear among its markets, and MX3D says its parts comply with ISO, API, ASME, DNV and Bureau Veritas guidelines.

## Integration Challenges

Distributed large-format metal printing is not plug-and-play.

**Quality assurance**: DNV publishes DNV-ST-B203, Additive manufacturing of metallic parts, which it describes as the first standard to provide an internationally accepted framework for producing and using metal additive parts in oil and gas, maritime and related industries. The level of quality assurance depends on how critical the part is. A 2022 edition added directed energy deposition using electric arc and laser beam, along with guidance on in-process monitoring.

**Post-processing**: WAAM parts need machining. A distributed cell needs that capability on hand. Hybrid machines that deposit and machine in one setup, like the DMG MORI machines and the system on USS Bataan, address that directly.

---

## Where This Leaves Operators

Large-format metal printing has been demonstrated on a bridge and a propeller, a metal printer has been installed on a warship, and a recognized qualification framework exists for energy and maritime use. The practical work for an operator is choosing which parts are worth qualifying and arranging the machining and inspection that printed parts still require.
